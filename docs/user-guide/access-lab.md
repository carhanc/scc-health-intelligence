# Using the Access Lab page

Access Lab shows how far a neighborhood is from care, by real walking and driving routes and by scheduled transit, and where a mobile-service site might reach the most people. It complements Explore's health/social profile with a specific focus on distance, travel time, and resource proximity.

## Finding a place

Type into "Find a place," the same search box as Explore — a city, district, or census tract number. Access Lab currently shows detailed results for **census tracts only**; searching a city or district shows guidance to pick a tract inside it instead, since the underlying travel-time and access computations are calculated per tract.

## Choosing walking or driving

The "Walking" / "Driving" control near the top switches which travel mode the Access summary tab shows. Scheduled transit access is always shown regardless of this choice, since it is its own separate mode.

## The four tabs

**Access summary** — once you select a tract, shows the modeled walking or driving distance and time to the nearest hospital and the nearest clinic, plus the nearest walkable transit stop and how frequently it's scheduled to run. Every figure states its method plainly ("Real walking-route distance," "Based on the published weekday schedule, not real-time arrivals") so you always know whether a number is a real measured route or a scheduled-service estimate. These figures describe the tract's largest-population sub-area (a real, specific location, not an average across the tract) — the page tells you exactly which one and how many people live there.

**Resource browser** — every hospital, clinic, food/SNAP retailer, and transit stop in the county, filterable by type, in a sortable, keyboard-accessible table. Each row shows whether it comes from an official source, whether it was confirmed by more than one source, and whether its location has been verified. This does not confirm current capacity, appointment availability, insurance acceptance, or language access — only that the facility, at this location, is a real entry in an official or supplemental public dataset.

**Resource gaps** — compares each tract's estimated health-burden level against its measured resource access, and shows where the two overlap: places with both high estimated need and low measured access ("priority gap"), places where need and access are both high or both low, and places with low need but high access ("well served"). This shows where a data pattern exists — it does not show that low access causes worse health, or that adding a resource would fix it. Select a tract to see its own percentile position on both dimensions.

**Mobile-service scenarios** — a set of pre-computed planning scenarios exploring where a small number of new mobile-service sites near existing transit stops could reach the most health-burden-weighted population, at different numbers of sites and different distance thresholds, with and without an equity requirement. These are **modeled planning explorations, not a decided plan** — a scenario showing "no solution satisfies these constraints" is a real, informative finding (not every combination of assumptions has an answer), never an error. Expand "Show assumptions and limitations" on any scenario to see exactly what it does and does not account for.

## What Access Lab cannot tell you

- Real-time transit arrivals, current traffic conditions, or road closures.
- Whether a specific facility is currently accepting patients, what insurance it takes, or what languages it serves in.
- Whether a health difference between two neighborhoods is *caused* by a difference in access — only that the two patterns overlap or don't.
- A confirmed plan for where a new service would be built — mobile-service scenarios are exploratory, not decisions.
